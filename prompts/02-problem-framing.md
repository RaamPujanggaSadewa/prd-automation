## Problem

Two to four paragraphs. State what is broken, for whom, and what it costs.

Use the specific findings from the brief. Do not generalise them into industry observations — if the brief says two adjacent shelves are visually identical but generated from different logic, say that, not "discovery lacks clarity". The specificity is the value; a statement that could describe any product in this category is worth nothing here.

Lead with the user's experience of the problem, then its consequence. Do not lead with the proposed solution.

## Context

One to two paragraphs. What surface this covers, which flow, and where it sits in the product. Establish the boundary of what is being discussed.

Then an explicit **Out of scope** list — three to six bullets naming things a reader might reasonably assume are included but are not. Take these from the brief's scope section. If the brief doesn't state a boundary, write `[NEEDS DATA] Scope boundary not defined in brief` rather than inventing one.

## Goals

Three to five goals, as a list. Each one states a change in user or business outcome, not a feature.

- Good: "Reduce the number of decisions a first-time user must make before reaching playback"
- Bad: "Add a simplified onboarding screen"

The second is a solution. Solutions belong in Requirements.

If a goal implies a measurable target, state the direction only. Do not attach a number unless the brief supplies a baseline.

## Rules

**Do not invent evidence.** No statistics, percentages, user counts, research findings, competitor data, revenue figures, or performance numbers unless they appear in the brief. This includes numbers that sound plausible or conservative. If a section reads better with a figure you don't have, write `[NEEDS DATA]` and describe what would need measuring.

**Do not invent user behaviour.** "Users frequently abandon" is a claim. If the brief says you observed it, cite it as an observation. If it doesn't, don't make the claim.

**Distinguish observation from inference.** If something is your reasoning rather than something observed, the wording should make that clear — "this suggests", "the likely result is" — rather than presenting it as established fact.

**Plain language.** No "leverage", "synergy", "seamless", "delight", "robust", "best-in-class", "empower", "streamline", "holistic". Write the way a person explains a problem to a colleague who will ask follow-up questions.

**No filler.** Every sentence should carry information. Delete anything that only restates the section heading or transitions without adding content.
